# Elsewhere Content Bible: Old Delhi Chapter (id `delhi`, flags `c11.`)

**Setting:** fictional mohalla of "Kucha Aab-o-Daana" (Urdu idiom: your bread and water, meaning the place fate feeds you), a knot of lanes off Chandni Chowk in the walled city of Shahjahanabad, Old Delhi. Real landmarks stay real at the edges: Sis Ganj Gurdwara, Fatehpuri Masjid, the Jama Masjid domes and Red Fort ramparts on the skyline, Khari Baoli at the west end. Shops and lanes inside the playable slice are fictional, the "San Isidro Guelazana" rule. **Season:** sawan, the Delhi monsoon, July into August. **Design creed:** culture is taught sideways, through food, words, and warm corrections. This chapter slots between Kerala (c6) and Zanzibar (c7). It is the game's loudest place and must feel like its warmest.

## 0. Getting there and away: the route verdict

**In:** rail from Kochi, three days north. Real trains run this line daily (Ernakulam to Delhi is roughly 2,700 km and 45 plus hours; the player's train takes three days with a monsoon delay, which is honest). The train itself is a corridor of India: chai vendors at every platform, languages changing station by station, the flat green of the Gangetic plain arriving on day three. The player steps out at a walled-city station a short rickshaw ride from the mohalla **[soft: Kerala trains actually terminate at Nizamuddin or New Delhi station; Delhi Junction by Chandni Chowk is a fictional convenience, keep the station unnamed]**. The monsoon is the connective tissue: the player leaves Kerala mid-rains and beats the monsoon north by a few days. Delhi's own onset normally lands around June 27. Mid-chapter, the first storm breaks over the rooftops and the whole mohalla comes out to stand in it. The player has now seen one weather system greet two worlds.

**Out:** rail south to Bombay overnight, then a working ship west across the Arabian Sea. This is the old dhow road under modern steel, and it keeps the Zanzibar bible's verdict intact: cargo ship crossing, dhows met as living craft on arrival, the monsoon still the road. The door out of Delhi is a chit, not a ticket (see section 10).

## 1. The playable slice: one gali as the spine

The mohalla is built like the real walled city: a spine and its organs.

- **Gali Tawe Wali** ("lane of the griddle"), the spine. Modeled on Paranthe Wali Gali, where the first parantha shop opened in 1872 and families from Agra turned a whole lane into one kitchen; of some 26 shops in the 1960s, a handful survive, run by the fourth and fifth generations. Our fictional lane has three shops, one chai corner, one jalebi kadhai at the mouth, and a wall of framed photos of famous eaters nobody can quite verify.
- **The chowk end**: the lane opens toward the great street. Painted backdrop, not walkable: Sis Ganj Gurdwara's golden dome (walkable interior, see section 4), the Jain temple's red sandstone with its bird hospital, a shikhara, a mosque gate, a church spire further down. All these faiths genuinely share this one street; play it as unremarked morning soundscape, bells and nitnem and azan layering, exactly like Kerala's three-faith dawn. Beyond, the skyline: Jama Masjid's domes, the Red Fort's long red wall. The main street is car-free by day (motor vehicles are banned 9 am to 9 pm since the 2021 revamp), so it belongs to feet, handcarts, and cycle-rickshaws.
- **The spice end**: the lane narrows west into a Khari Baoli style corridor. Khari Baoli is Asia's largest wholesale spice market, trading since the 1600s beside Fatehpuri Masjid (both named for Fatehpuri Begum, a wife of Shah Jahan), with shops on their ninth and tenth generations. Sack mountains, porters with impossible loads, and the famous involuntary sneezing as chilli dust rides the air **[soft: the sneezing corridor is travel-writing lore, well attested informally; play it as a running gag]**.
- **The rooftop level**: a second map above the first. Connected terraces, water tanks, dhobi lines, a kabootar khana (pigeon coop), kite spools, monkeys commuting along the wire bundles, and the only place in the mohalla where the sky is big. Old Delhi lives a second life up here.
- **Interiors**: Kamla Chachi's shop kitchen; the langar hall; a quiet poet's haveli room (section 7); the attar shop.

## 2. Sawan, the season

July in Delhi is the exhale after the furnace. Everyone still talks about the garmi that just ended, because Dilli ki garmi and Dilli ki sardi are both civic achievements locals brag about. Then the rain: sudden, warm, theatrical, streets ankle-deep in twenty minutes, chai sales tripling, someone always frying pakoras because rain without pakoras is a wasted rain. Sawan is also mango season's peak. Crates of langra and chausa perfume whole lanes, and Ghalib gives the chapter its mango theology (section 7). And sawan is kite weather: the monsoon breeze is the patang's engine, and the season builds toward August 15, when Delhi's rooftops turn into a dueling sky. The chapter's finale rides this without pinning a date: the mohalla holds its own rooftop tournament.

**What sawan is not:** daulat ki chaat season. This is the chapter's flagship warm correction. Daulat ki chaat is Old Delhi's winter ghost: milk churned to foam outdoors on cold nights, set by dew and moonlight, saffron and khoya on top, sold off khomchas (wicker-stand platters) from November to February and physically impossible in warm weather; the foam will not hold. The player, primed by Nani's journal, asks for it in July. The halwai laughs kindly: "Sawan mein? The moon is behind clouds, beta. Come back when your breath shows." He gives rabri instead and a promise. Nani was promised the same thing in 1974 and never collected. Two generations, one IOU held by a sweet. Do not let the player eat it; the game's best gifts are the ones the world declines to give.

## 3. Food, the main teacher

- **Paranthas:** the lane's shops fry them in desi ghee in shallow iron kadhais, crisp-shelled and flaky, and the old families keep pure vegetarian kitchens, no onion, no garlic. Classic stuffings: aloo, gobhi, paneer, mooli; house legends run to banana, khoya, rabri, and mixed dry fruit. Served the fixed old way with the same sides for every parantha: sweet banana-tamarind chutney, mint chutney, aloo-methi sabzi, pickle **[soft: exact side lineup varies by shop; keep our fictional shop's lineup consistent instead]**. This is the cooking verb (section 9).
- **Jalebi:** the model is the 1884 shop at the Dariba corner, four generations squeezing thick jalebis into desi ghee and drowning them in syrup, best eaten scalding with rabri. Ours is "Bade Mian Jalebi Wale, since 1902," fictional, corner position, one kadhai, no menu.
- **Breakfast canon:** bedmi puri with aloo sabzi, nagori halwa, khasta kachori with sabzi, chole bhature that make lunch unnecessary, chole kulche off a handcart. Breakfast in the walled city is a public event that begins before seven.
- **Chaat:** dahi bhalle bumpy with pomegranate, aloo tikki off the tawa. Chaat is argument food; two NPCs disagree forever about whose lane does it right.
- **Cold and sweet:** matka kulfi cut in coins, faluda optional; shikanji (nimbu soda, the salt-sugar-lime fix for heat); and sherbet, above all Rooh Afza, the rose-herb syrup invented in Old Delhi in 1907 at Lal Kuan as a summer coolant, pink as a wedding, poured into milk or ice water. An NPC calls it "the mohalla's blood type."
- **Chai:** in kulhads, the unglazed clay cups that add petrichor to every sip and shatter musically when done. The kulhad is the chapter's cup and a quiet rhyme with mitti attar (section 7): in Delhi even the dishes taste of rain.
- **The other kitchen:** toward the Jama Masjid side, the mohalla knows nihari pots sealed overnight and kebab smoke in the evening, the walled city's Muslim food canon. The parantha lane is vegetarian; the city is not one kitchen. One line of honest texture, no tourism montage.
- **Langar:** the most important food in the chapter costs nothing and is eaten on the floor (section 4).

## 4. The langar (the chapter's soul)

Sis Ganj Gurdwara stands where the ninth Sikh Guru, Guru Tegh Bahadur, was beheaded on Aurangzeb's orders in 1675 for defending another community's freedom of faith; Sikhs first built here in 1783. State this once, plainly, through the sevadar; it is why the kitchen never closes its heart.

The langar is the community kitchen: every gurdwara feeds everyone, free, vegetarian, no questions. The etiquette is real and the game teaches it by warm correction, never by scoring:

- Shoes off at the counter, head covered (a basket of rumals waits by the door for anyone without).
- Everyone sits on the floor in rows called pangat: no chairs, no sections, no rich or poor, no Sikh or non-Sikh. A CEO and a porter eat the same dal at the same level.
- Receive with both hands cupped. Head stays covered while eating.
- You do not pay. When the player offers money, the sevadar folds it back gently: "Not coins, beta. Hands. Come Tuesday, you roll rotis."
- Seva is the answer: anyone may serve. The player kneads atta, rolls rotis, wipes the floor, stacks plates. No skill required, which is the point.

This is the chapter's journal flagship. The rhyme is written to chapter one: langar seva is ayni with the ledger erased. Ayni remembers who owes whom; the langar deliberately forgets, so that no one is ever a debtor at dinner. Nani's rhyme note carries it (section 10). The langar scene is scripted and unscored: no minigame inside the gurdwara, ever.

## 5. The rooftop republic: pigeons and kites

- **Kabootarbazi** is the Mughal-descended art of keeping and flying pigeon flocks from rooftop coops; Babur's own memoirs mention it. Flocks rise at dawn and dusk and wheel over the walled city; ustads call them home with personal languages of whistles and syllables no two keepers share, and a trained flock can seduce a rival's stray bird into landing with them. The big wager tournaments belong to winter **[soft: season per Old Delhi reporting; daily flying is year-round]**, so in sawan our ustad flies for love, not stakes. His apprentices are shahgirds; he answers only to the dead ustad who taught him.
- **Patangbazi** is the dueling kite art: paper patangs, the spool (charkhi), and the two verbs of flight, dheel (give slack) and kheench (pull). Rival strings cross; the sharper line saws the other; the sky shouts "woh kata!" (that one's cut). Glass-coated manjha cuts birds and hands, and Chinese synthetic manjha has been banned in Delhi since 2017; the ustad flies plain cotton dor and says the sky has enough blood in it. His pigeons and the Jain bird hospital across the street (real: the Digambar Jain temple by the Red Fort runs a free charity hospital for injured birds) make this a lived ethic, not a disclaimer.
- Dusk on the roofs is the chapter's signature image: pigeon flocks wheeling, kites climbing between them, monkeys retreating along the wires, the Jama Masjid domes going rose-colored, thunderheads stacking behind the Red Fort.

## 6. Words and warmth

The player absorbs, never studies:

- **haan ji**: yes, upholstered with respect. The mohalla's metronome.
- **bhaiya / didi**: brother, sister, the default address for strangers, and the game's warmth mechanic again: NPCs soften when the player uses it. The journal rhymes it with Kerala's chetta and chechi: every place the player has walked gives family names to strangers.
- **aur batao**: "tell me more," the second hello. It means the conversation has no agenda and no clock.
- **thoda aur lo / pet bhar gaya**: "take a little more" versus "my stomach is full." The dance: a first refusal is never accepted, a second refusal is barely accepted, and one more jalebi lands on your plate regardless. Here, "na" means "convince me."
- **jugaad**: the frugal fix, the workaround with dignity. A rickshaw running on three unrelated parts is jugaad; so is a rooftop antenna guyed with kite string.
- **mehmaan-nawazi**: hospitality as identity; the guest outranks the host's convenience. Its Hindi twin proverb, atithi devo bhava (the guest is god), gets quoted at the player by someone refusing payment.
- **abhi / bas paanch minute**: elastic time. "Right now" spans this hour and possibly this week. Journal rhyme forward to Oaxaca's ahorita and back to Swahili time: three coasts, one negotiable clock.
- **Dilli dilwalon ki**: "Delhi belongs to the big-hearted," the city's own slogan for itself, best delivered by someone doing you an unreasonable favor.
- **Hinglish**: "scene kya hai?", "tension mat lo," "done ho jayega." The mohalla's teenagers speak three languages per sentence and correct nobody.
- **Bargaining as affection**: in the market lanes the first price is an opening of negotiations, not an insult, and paying it instantly is faintly rude, like refusing to dance. The walk-away is a step in the choreography; the call-back ("arre suniye toh") is the hug.
- **Sardi-garmi talk**: complaining about Delhi weather is civic bonding. Agreeing enthusiastically is how outsiders get adopted.

Old Delhi is Hindi and Urdu leaning on the same counter: signboards run three deep in three scripts (Devanagari, Nastaliq, Latin), and the mohalla is Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and Jain in adjacent doorways. Play it as neighbors with keys to each other's shops, not as a message.

## 7. The market ends and the poet's lane

- **Khari Baoli (spice end):** the Kerala thread lands here. The pepper and cardamom the player learned on the backwaters arrive in these sacks; a ninth-generation trader can name a spice's home district by smell. This is the recall consumption scene: the trader tests the player with a smell test and is delighted that a firangi kid knows small cardamom from big. His ledgers reach Bombay, and Bombay's ships once reached Zanzibar; his chit is the road west (section 10).
- **Dariba Kalan model (silver and attar):** a lane of silver dealers, the country's oldest bullion street, and attar shops on the Gulab Singh Johrimal model (perfumers since 1816). The chapter's perfume is **mitti attar**: baked earth discs from Kannauj hydro-distilled into sandalwood oil, literally petrichor in a bottle, the smell of first rain sold by the tola. Mehr Aapa's gift of a tiny vial is the chapter keepsake: Delhi bottles its monsoon, and in Zanzibar the same nose will meet cloves drying on mats.
- **Kinari and Chawri glimpses:** a wedding-card lane (Chawri Bazar is the real paper and card market) and a trims lane of gota, garlands, and groom turbans (Kinari Bazar model). Background economy, one errand deep.
- **The poet's lane (Ballimaran model):** Ghalib, the greatest Urdu poet of Delhi, lived his last years in a haveli in Gali Qasim Jan, Ballimaran; part of it is now a small memorial museum with his couplets on the walls. Our fictional "haveli of the hakims' lane" holds a caretaker, a courtyard, and two real couplets used with love: "hazaaron khwahishen aisi ki har khwahish pe dam nikle" (a thousand desires, each worth a life; many came true, and still too few), and "dil-e-nadaan tujhe hua kya hai" (oh innocent heart, what has happened to you). The chai-wallah outside runs a standing offer: recite any sher, get chai at friend price. Ghalib's mango doctrine is canon: asked what he required of mangoes, he said they should be sweet and they should be many; when a friend observed that even donkeys refuse mangoes, Ghalib agreed: exactly, even donkeys. Sawan mangoes make this scene play itself.

## 8. Cast sketch (eight, plus the standing devices)

1. **Kamla Chachi**, fourth-generation parantha maker, Gali Tawe Wali. Runs the tawa like a courtroom. Teaches the parantha verb. Corrects rolling technique by taking your hands in hers. Her rule: "The gali feeds first, asks later." Packs the departure paranthas.
2. **Bantu**, sixteen, cycle-rickshaw apprentice and self-appointed guide. Full Hinglish. The player's first friend. He is the chapter's person who is quietly wrong about his own culture: swears Chandni Chowk means "silver street" because of the jewelers; the sevadar corrects him gently: moonlight square, for the moon in the old canal.
3. **Ustad Yusuf Miyan**, kabootarbaz and kite-maker, king of the rooftop. Gruff, poetic, flies cotton dor only. Teaches the kite verb. Speaks to pigeons in a private language and to humans reluctantly. Softens when the player learns the birds' names.
4. **Sardar Joginder Singh**, sevadar at the gurdwara langar. Enormous, calm, floury to the elbows. Delivers the langar corrections and the chapter's soul line: "In ayni you remember who owes. Here we wash the ledger with the dishes."
5. **Mehr-un-Nisa, "Mehr Aapa"**, attar seller, Dariba model lane. Blends by memory, speaks in half-couplets, sells nothing to people she dislikes. Gives the mitti attar vial when the player describes Kerala's first rain accurately. Keeper of the Nani thread: her mother once sold perfume to a Peruvian girl with a journal **[fictional, in-game]**.
6. **Sethji Onkar Nath**, ninth-generation Khari Baoli trader. The barrier with personality. Sneezing porters orbit him. Tests strangers by smell. His chit opens the road to Bombay and the ship west. He is also the chapter's person who does not care that you exist, until the cardamom moment.
7. **Sushila Jain**, volunteer at the bird hospital. Patient hands, zero sentimentality. Patches a manjha-cut pigeon in front of the player, which is why our tournament flies cotton. Disagrees with Yusuf about whether kites should fly at all; they have argued for thirty years and share tea daily.
8. **Akhtar Bhai**, chai-wallah at the gali mouth, kulhad specialist, Ghalib-drunk. Runs "sher for chai." Announces the rain like a sports commentator. Delivers the daulat ki chaat correction alongside the halwai beat if a ninth NPC is too many.

**Standing devices:** Chasca appears at the kite tournament dusk and takes one photo (flag `photo.c11.kites`): the sky full of kites and pigeons as the first big storm walks in and nobody leaves the roofs. Pilar's letter waits at the mohalla post office: it is election day on the bridge, electorate of nine, currently tied four to four because the ninth voter has gone fishing; she is confident, and she invoices the suspense. At least one letter variant must react to a real Kerala flag (the sadya, the rowing seat, or Joseph's letter delivered).

## 9. Verbs, frictions, finale

**Verb one, parantha assembly (cooking verb):** taught by Kamla Chachi. Roll the disc, cup the stuffing, seal without tearing, roll again thin but unbroken, onto the kadhai, flip on sound cues, out golden. Difficulty rises with fancier stuffings; the rabri parantha is the graduation. Success is measured in a customer's silence.

**Verb two, patangbazi (rooftop verb):** taught by Ustad Yusuf. Read the wind, climb with dheel, hold against gusts, cross a rival's line and saw with kheench. Pigeon flocks cross the sky at intervals; the player must give ground when birds pass (Yusuf's one law, and the game's). Finale difficulty: the tournament, three rivals, rising monsoon gusts.

**Frictions (warm corrections, all researched):**
1. **Daulat ki chaat in sawan.** Wrong season, gorgeously refused. A promise for December, twice-inherited (Nani holds the same IOU).
2. **Langar etiquette.** Money folded back into the player's hand; head, shoes, floor, both hands cupped; corrected with total warmth. Repayment is seva on Tuesday, which becomes an errand.
3. **The refusal dance.** The player says no to a second helping and gets it anyway; the journal teaches "pet bhar gaya" plus the stomach pat, and even that earns one more jalebi. Bonus friction for the market: paying the first price and being chased down the lane, because the shopkeeper's honor requires giving something back.

**Barrier with personality:** Sethji Onkar Nath. "In this market nothing moves without a chit, and no chit was ever written for a stranger." Becoming not-a-stranger is the chapter: Kamla vouches that you can feed people, Joginder that you can serve them, Yusuf that you can read the wind. Then the smell test: the player, carrying Kerala's spice knowledge, names the cardamom's coast. Sethji writes the chit to his Bombay shipping cousin whose firm has loaded for Zanzibar since his grandfather's day, and throws in a clove order to carry, because no traveler leaves his market empty-handed.

**Finale:** the sawan rooftop tournament at dusk. Yusuf calls his flock down first so the sky belongs to paper. The player flies for the mohalla. Mid-duel the storm arrives; kites come down, kulhads come out, the roofs stay full, everyone soaked and shouting. Chasca's shutter. Next morning: last langar, Kamla's parantha bundle with mango pickle for the train, Bantu's rickshaw to the station, the chit in the player's pocket. Rail south, then the sea road west.

- **Nani, 1974 (canon allows entries here; she reached this page):** five entries, thoughtful register, monsoon-dated. She rode a tonga from the station (trams were already gone). ## 11. Sensory palette for the map artist

Tangled power-line bundles sagging between havelis, thick as anacondas. Signboards stacked three deep in three scripts, half of them hand-painted. Carved wooden jharokha balconies leaning over aluminum shopfronts; a marble arch above a plastic awning. Cycle-rickshaws with tinsel and painted flowers; handcarts (thela) of mangoes, marigold garland sellers threading at dawn. Sack pyramids and chilli haze at the spice end; silver glinting in dim shop caves; wedding cards fanned in windows; attar bottles in amber rows. Ghee drums, the jalebi kadhai's copper glow, kulhads stacked in clay towers, khomcha baskets. Rooftops: water tanks, dhobi lines, kite spools, the kabootar khana, monkeys on the wires, pigeons wheeling at dusk. Rain: tarpaulins bloom over stalls, lanes mirror neon, kids commandeer puddles. Sound: rickshaw bells, the azan and the gurdwara's kirtan and temple bells in layers, "woh kata!" from the roofs, thunder with theatrical timing, the hiss of ghee. Smell: rain on hot stone (the mitti attar note), frying paranthas, chilli dust, marigolds, wet clay cups.

## 12. Traps (hard rules)

1. No poverty porn and no "chaos" exoticism: the crowd has an order locals read fluently; the player learns to read it too.
2. No "India" monolith and no Kerala reprise: this is Hindustani-speaking, wheat-eating, landlocked north India; let NPCs tease the difference.
3. The gurdwara is sacred ground: documented etiquette only, no minigame, no score, no romance beats inside; seva is scripted and unhurried.
4. Mixed Hindu-Muslim-Sikh-Jain life is shown as neighbors and keys, never as a sermon and never erased; no Partition lecture, one elder memory at most.
5. No real shop names verbatim; landmarks real, businesses fictional.
6. Monkeys and street dogs are managed neighbors, not comedy props.
7. Kites: the manjha harm to birds is acknowledged in-world (cotton dor canon); do not glamorize glass string.
8. Bollywood exists here as texture (posters, a ringtone), not as identity.
9. Heat and rain are real but never miserabilist; sawan is relief and celebration.
10. Nobody speaks "for Delhi"; the chaat argument proves it.
