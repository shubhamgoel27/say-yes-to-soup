# Elsewhere Content Bible: Valles Centrales, Oaxaca
## Chapter: "The Path of Petals" (late October, Día de los Muertos)

**Premise anchor:** The player arrives in a fictional Valles Centrales village with their grandmother's half-finished 1974 travel journal. Thesis: the dead are not gone if someone sets a place for them. Fictional village, real texture. Nothing should feel like a costume.

---

## 1. Día de los Muertos, done right

**It is a reunion, not a mourning.** The dead are invited home, fed, gossiped with, teased. The cemetery night is candlelit, warm, often funny. Write scenes where laughter and tears share a graveside.

**The calendar matters:**
- **Oct 31:** altars are finished; by evening the arrival begins. Markets peak in the days before, overflowing with flowers, sugar skulls, bread.
- **Nov 1:** the **angelitos**, children who died, are received first. Small sweets, toys, tamales without chile. Handle with extreme tenderness; never play it for spectacle.
- **Nov 2:** the adult dead. The cemetery vigil, mole on the altar, mezcal poured for those who drank it.

**The ofrenda/altar:** commonly two levels (heaven and earth), three (heaven, purgatory, earth), or seven (the levels the soul climbs). Elements and their jobs: **cempasúchil** marigolds whose color and scent guide the soul, with a **petal path** laid from street to altar; **copal** resin smoke to purify and carry prayers; **water** for the thirsty traveler; **salt**; **candles** to light the way; **photographs**; **pan de muerto**; and crucially **the dead person's favorite things**: her cigarettes, his mezcal, her mole, a deck of cards. Gameplay hook: building Grandma's altar requires knowing, from the whole game, what she loved.

**Oaxacan pan de muerto** is its own thing: egg-rich **pan de yema** decorated with a small painted face, the **carita**, pressed into the crown. Oaxaca is the region where the bread looks back at you. Dunked in chocolate.

**Comparsas and muerteadas:** costumed processions with brass bands, dancing, satire, mezcal, moving house to house through the night; the Etla valley's **muerteadas** run nearly twenty-four hours. The holiday's sanctioned wild joy; contrast with the vigil's quiet.

**Not "Mexican Halloween."** Different roots (Mesoamerican observance layered onto Catholic All Saints/All Souls), different register: relationship with the dead, not fear of them. Halloween imagery does bleed in at the edges in real life; the game can note this honestly without endorsing the conflation.

**The Coco effect (honest note):** Pixar's *Coco* (2017) was carefully researched and beloved by many Mexicans, and it also supercharged Day of the Dead tourism. Researchers document "temporal overtourism" at Mexican celebration sites: crowded cemeteries, spectacle over meaning, locals adapting. Let an NPC say this plainly, and let the player choose to be a guest, not an audience.

## 2. Guelaguetza, the concept (the chapter's thesis)

**Verified:** Guelaguetza is a Zapotec system of **reciprocal exchange and mutual aid** in the Valles Centrales: when a family faces a wedding, funeral, or fiesta, others contribute goods, money, labor. These contributions are **remembered, often literally written in notebooks, as debts of kindness** returned when the giver's own occasion comes, sometimes across generations. Related Zapotec term: *guendalezaa*, "offering, gift, cooperation." Core idea: "today for you, tomorrow for me."

**Distinguish it from the July festival.** The state-sponsored Guelaguetza festival (Cerro del Fortín, two Mondays in July) is a 20th-century appropriation of the word into a folkloric showcase. Our chapter is October; the festival exists only as a mention. We dramatize the living system.

**Series payoff:** the chain of Quechua **ayni** (reciprocal labor), coastal **yapa** (the vendor's little extra), and Korean **jeong** (accumulated attachment) culminates here. Guelaguetza is the thesis: reciprocity made formal, recorded, multigenerational. **Climax candidate:** the village's guelaguetza notebook holds a 1974 entry. Grandma gave something. The village still owes her, and pays the debt to the player: help building her altar. Remembering the dead and owing the living are the same ledger.

## 3. Markets

- **Tianguis:** open-air markets rotating by weekday between towns (Nahuatl *tianquiztli*), a pre-Hispanic institution. Real anchors: Tlacolula on Sundays, Ocotlán on Fridays, Etla on Wednesdays. Give the fictional village its market day.
- **Marchanta/marchante:** what vendors and regulars call each other, the Mexican equivalent of Peru's *casera* bond (etymology likely French *marchand*; soft claim). "¡Pásele, marchanta!" is the classic call.
- **El pilón:** **verified** as the Mexican "little extra" a vendor adds free for a regular, cognate in function to yapa and Louisiana's lagniappe (from *pilón*, a cone of sugar once thrown in). Direct mechanical rhyme with the earlier yapa system.
- **Mercado eating:** memelas and tlayudas made at the comal, eaten standing or at long shared benches.

## 4. Food

- **Mole negro:** king of Oaxaca's seven moles. Around thirty ingredients; charred **chilhuacle negro** chiles (grown almost only in La Cañada, scarce and costly), plus mulato and pasilla oaxaqueño, nuts, raisins, spices, burnt tortilla, chocolate. Preparation spans days. **Occasion food**: weddings, funerals, Day of the Dead. Grandma's journal should mention a mole she never finished learning.
- **Tejate:** pre-Hispanic Zapotec drink of nixtamalized corn, cacao, toasted mamey seed (**pixtle**), and **rosita de cacao** flower, hand-worked by women called **tejateras** (San Andrés Huayapam is its heartland) until white foam rises. Drunk cold from painted jícaras. The foam is the prize.
- **Chocolate de agua** (hot chocolate beaten frothy in water, not milk), **chapulines** (toasted grasshoppers with lime and chile, sold by the scoop), **quesillo** (the true name of "Oaxaca cheese"), **tamales oaxaqueños** steamed flat in banana leaf with mole negro, and **atole** at dawn.
- **Mezcal, briefly and respectfully:** sipped, never shot, often with orange and worm salt. The patience theme: espadín matures in six to eight years, wild tobalá ten to fifteen, tepeztate up to twenty-five or more. A plant older than the player, drunk in one evening, poured for the dead.

## 5. Craft (all real villages; keep them real, our village is separate)

- **Barro negro**, San Bartolo Coyotepec: **verified** that in the 1950s **Doña Rosa (Rosa Real Mateo de Nieto)** developed the technique of burnishing near-dry clay with a quartz stone and firing lower, turning matte grey ware shiny black. A woman's hands changed the whole craft. Journal beat: Grandma may have watched her work (she died in 1980, so 1974 is plausible; flag as fictionalized encounter).
- **Alebrijes:** **recent** art, not ancient. Pedro Linares, a Mexico City cartonero, dreamed the creatures during a fever around 1936 and built them in papier-mâché; Oaxacan carvers (Manuel Jiménez of Arrazola first) translated them into **copal wood**, now the pride of San Antonio Arrazola and San Martín Tilcajete. Let an NPC gently puncture the "ancient spirit animal" myth.
- **Weaving**, Teotitlán del Valle: Zapotec weavers, wool tapestry looms, natural dyes. **Cochineal red**: carminic acid from an insect raised on nopal cactus, once colonial "red gold." **The rhyme is verified at both ends:** cochineal originated in the Andes and dyed pre-Columbian Peruvian textiles (common from the Huari/Tiwanaku era), and Peru remains the world's top producer today, while Oaxaca made it an empire's export. The same crushed insect reddens Grandma's Peruvian chapter and this one.

## 6. Zapotec, alive

Valley Zapotec greeting: **"Padiuxi"** (also padiuxh/padiuxhi), a hello said warmly. "Guelaguetza" itself is the Zapotec word players have been circling. Portray honestly: hundreds of thousands speak Zapotec languages, yet many Valley variants are endangered as child transmission thins; pride and revival efforts (community classes, the Ticha project) coexist with loss. An elder who speaks it and a grandchild learning it back is truthful.

---

## 7. Language by ear (Spanish)

- **¡Provecho!** said to anyone eating, including strangers; answer "gracias, igualmente."
- **¿Mande?** the Mexican "what?/pardon?"
- **Ahorita:** elastic time, from "right now" to "eventually, maybe." Rhymes with Peru's *al toque*.
- **Ándale** (all-purpose: right!, hurry!, exactly). **Qué padre** is broadly Mexican; in Oaxaca you will also hear **qué chulo** (soft claim on exact regional distribution; safe as flavor).
- **Sobremesa:** the long lingering at the table after eating, verified as a living Mexican custom; leaving right after the meal reads as coldness.

---

## 8. Texture

Cempasúchil fields glowing orange before the fiesta; papel picado strung across streets, shivering; **cohetes**, rockets fired at random hours (bang, no spectacle, dogs complain); the village banda practicing scales at dusk; bougainvillea over adobe walls; green **cantera** stone (Oaxaca City's churches are carved from it); burros on back roads; wood-and-copal smoke; the high dry valley light that cuts shadows sharp at 1,500 meters.

---

## 9. Traps (hard rules)

1. No skeleton face paint on the player as tourist default; if offered, an NPC notes it is for comparsas, not for photographing graves.
2. No Halloween framing, no "spooky" scoring.
3. **No mariachi.** Oaxaca is banda de viento territory.
4. **No tequila.** This is mezcal country.
5. No Frida-ification; she is Mexico City, another era.
6. Sugar skulls only with names written on the forehead and given with meaning, never as kitsch set dressing.
7. Real craft villages stay real; our village never claims their inventions.

**Flagged soft claims:** written guelaguetza notebooks (well attested ethnographically but practice varies by village); marchante etymology; qué chulo regionality; seven-level altar symbolism varies household to household; Doña Rosa encounter is our fiction.
