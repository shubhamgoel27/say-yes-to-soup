# Content Bible: A Highland Village in the Peruvian Sierra
### Sourced raw material for game writing (Cusco/Puno altiplano, 3,500-4,000m)
*Produced by a research pass on 2026-07-20. Every non-obvious claim carries a source link. The village and people in the game remain fictional; this file is texture, not a portrait of any real community.*

**Orthography note.** Quechua here is southern (Cusco-Collao) Quechua. Two spelling systems compete: the official 3-vowel system (a, i, u) and the Cusco Academy's 5-vowel system. Sources mix them; forms given are what a player would hear. If a translator is hired (they should be), hire a Cusco-Collao speaker, not Ayacucho-Chanka, and pick one orthography.

## 1. Daily Rhythm

**Pre-dawn.** The highest herding households (Chillihuani, 3,800-5,000m) begin work around 4:00 a.m.; a mixed farming village at 3,500-3,800m wakes closer to 5-6 a.m., "with the roosters" ([Cultural Survival](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/quechua-guardians-potato); [Middlebury field diary](https://sites.middlebury.edu/amandabensel/2012/08/28/a-day-in-the-life-peru-edition/)). The woman of the house relights the fire from the night's embers, sometimes blowing through a length of bamboo onto smoldering dung coals ([Smithsonian, Pacchanta](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/weaving-women-ausangate-peru-textiles-tradition-quechua-identity-180956468/)). Patio swept, animals fed, water boiled or carried before anyone eats.

**Meals.** Four: desayuno, almuerzo (the main meal), lonche (late-afternoon), and a light comida after dark. Breakfast is large and usually a hot soup built from last night's leftovers plus potatoes, because the morning's work runs for hours without a break ([Enigma](https://www.enigmaperu.com/blog/traditional-breakfast-foods-of-peru/)). Lunch is soup first, then a segundo of potatoes/mote/chuño with a little cheese, egg, or meat. Dinner is leftovers or another soup.

**Coca punctuates the day like a clock.** In Sonqo (Paucartambo, 3,900m), adults chew coca about five times daily: after breakfast, mid-morning (~10 a.m.), after the midday meal, mid-afternoon (~3 p.m.), and after dinner. The mid-morning and mid-afternoon breaks are called *hallpay* and function like the coffee break; nobody works during hallpay, and nobody interrupts it (Allen 1981, [PDF](https://ipearqueologia.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/allen-quechua.pdf)).

**Who does what.** Men lead heavy chacra work and community leadership; women manage the household, herding, spinning, seed selection, and small animals, but the roles are an interdependent partnership, not a strict hierarchy ([Allen](https://archive.org/details/holdlifehascoca00alle); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2022.787600/full)). Children work young and are proud of it: a girl gets a spindle at age three and spins usable yarn by five or six; kids of 5-8 take sheep to near pasture; by twelve a child manages the family herd and assists at animal births ([Spin Off](https://spinoffmagazine.com/spinning-from-cradle-to-grave-andean-spinning/); [Bolin, *Growing Up in a Culture of Respect*](https://ebin.pub/growing-up-in-a-culture-of-respect-child-rearing-in-highland-peru-9780292795976.html)). Women spin while walking, herding, and sitting in meetings. Grandparents increasingly do the day-shepherding, sometimes paid in grazing rights ([PASTRES](https://pastres.org/2023/02/24/the-futures-of-the-pastoralist-systems-of-southern-andean-peru/)).

**The herding day.** Animals out of the stone corral at dawn, walked to pasture (bofedal wetlands in the dry season heights), home at dusk. Herds run 100-300 head; the herder carries a warm lunch of potatoes wrapped in a cloth, a sling (waraka), and a spindle ([Panos](https://library.panos.co.uk/features/stories/alpaqueros.html); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2015/6/25/pulling-the-wool-over-peruvian-shepherds-eyes)).

**The year.** Planting begins with the September full moon (a three-person team on the chaquitaclla foot plow: one levers the sod, one turns it, one drops seed and llama dung); rains November-April; the lean months are roughly December-February when last year's stores run low; harvest April-June; watia earth ovens and chuño-making in the frosts of June-July; fallow and fiestas after. Communal land rotates by sector (the turno/laymi system); the assembly sets planting dates in September ([Frontiers, turno](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2025.1702404/full); [Ayni Peru](https://ayni-peru.com/peru-agricultural-calendar/); [Agro-Insight](https://www.agroinsight.com/blog/?p=5377)).

**Market day vs. the week.** Once a week the family walks (sometimes hours) to the district feria (Pisac and Chinchero are the famous models), carrying potatoes, chuño, cheese, and wool to sell or barter (trueque is still live alongside cash), and comes home with salt, sugar, noodles, coca, and news. Market day is social infrastructure: kin met, chicha drunk, ayni arranged ([Best of Peru Travel](https://bestofperutravel.com/place/pisac-market/)). The other institutional day is the asamblea comunal: all registered comuneros, attendance enforced by fines (multas) under the community's own statute ([Ley 24656](https://www2.congreso.gob.pe/sicr/cendocbib/con3_uibd.nsf/1DAB0BF2E43B8FBB0525797B006DE3C0/$FILE/1_LEY_24656_Ley_General_Comunidades_Campesinas_SPIJ.pdf)).

**Evening.** The family gathers in the warm, smoky kitchen; soup, muña or coca tea; radio (much of it Quechua-language; the national Quechua newscast Ñuqanchik airs at 5:30 a.m.) ([Peru Quechua Media](https://peruquechuamedia.wordpress.com/2012/12/05/radio-quechua/); [TVPerú](https://tvperu.gob.pe/noticias/regionales/tv-peru-y-radio-nacional-estrenaron-noticiero-en-quechua-nuqanchik)). Lighting is a bare bulb on a small solar system, or candles; people joke about "switching on the lightbulb to look for the candle" ([Borgen](https://borgenproject.org/plant-powered-lamp-lights-up-peruvian-villages/)). Bed early, the whole family often in one room, infants sleeping beside the mother on sheepskins until age four or five ([Bolin](https://ebin.pub/growing-up-in-a-culture-of-respect-child-rearing-in-highland-peru-9780292795976.html)).

## 2. Food, Concretely

**Papa (potato).** Peru grows roughly 2,800-3,500 native varieties; Cusco alone about 800; a single conservationist family may plant 120-200 varieties in one field ([CIP](https://cipotato.org/potato/native-potato-varieties/); [Cultural Survival](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/quechua-guardians-potato)). The names are jokes and poems: **puma maki** (puma's paw), **yana wayru** (a prized ceremonial purple-red), **wira pasña** ("curvy young woman"), **puka ñawi pasña** ("red-eyed girl"), and the famous **llumchuy waqachi**, "makes-the-daughter-in-law-cry": a knobbly, deep-eyed tuber a prospective mother-in-law hands the bride with a knife. Peel it thinly and cleanly or be judged wasteful. Women really cried ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sociology/articles/10.3389/fsoc.2023.1268445/full); [Crop Trust](https://www.croptrust.org/news-events/news/potato-recipes-women-potato-park/)). Everyday eating: a mound of boiled potatoes, peeled by hand, dipped in uchukuta. In harvest season, the **watia**: a dome of dry earth clods built in the field, fired inside until the clods glow, then collapsed over the potatoes to bake them for 30-40 minutes; eaten at the field edge with cheese and ají, a harvest thanks to Pachamama ([Huatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huatia); [Cuzco Eats](https://cuzcoeats.com/may-brings-abundance-and-huatia-for-all/)).

**Chuño and moraya.** June-July, above ~3,800m: small bitter potatoes spread on flat ground, frozen through minus 5°C nights and dried in the day sun, three nights or more; then the family treads them barefoot in a pile, "dancing" the water and skins out. Black chuño is then just sun-dried; for white moraya/tunta the trodden potatoes soak in a cold stream pool for two to four weeks and bleach white in the sun. Stores for a decade or more ([Chuño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu%C3%B1o); [Cuzco Eats](https://cuzcoeats.com/cold-season-and-the-arrival-of-chuno-and-moraya/)). Honest tasting note: rehydrated black chuño is spongy-chewy, earthy, faintly sour and mineral; outsiders say "chalk" or "Styrofoam"; people raised on it call it comfort ([ZME](https://www.zmescience.com/medicine/nutrition-medicine/chuno-taters/)). It is why the lean months are survivable, and it was Inca tribute currency.

**Quinoa (and kañiwa).** Washed repeatedly to rinse off bitter saponins until the water stops foaming; eaten in the highlands mostly as a light sopa de quinua, not as a fluffy grain side (that framing is urban). Kañiwa needs no washing and survives where quinoa fails; toasted into nutty kañihuaco flour. The quinoa boom tripled prices; studies in Puno found growers kept eating it, but the urban poor got squeezed ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X18302419)).

**Ají / uchu.** Rocoto (apple-shaped, black seeds, hot), ají amarillo (fruity, the backbone), ají panca (dried, smoky-mild). The daily sauce is **uchukuta**: chilies, huacatay (a pungent marigold-mint), salt, sometimes cheese, ground fresh on the **batán**, a flat base stone and a crescent rocker stone worked with body weight, the pre-Hispanic food processor ([Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/1452432/types-peruvian-peppers-explained/); [Taste Atlas](https://www.tasteatlas.com/uchucuta)).

**Cuy.** Twenty or so guinea pigs live loose in the kitchen, fed alfalfa and peels, tended by women and children; a house without them is thought lazy or destitute. Fiesta meat, not everyday: baptisms, first haircuts, All Souls, honored guests. Roasted whole (Cusco) or fried flat under a stone (chactado, Arequipa); tastes like a cross between rabbit and duck, all crispy skin and small bones, eaten with the hands. Diners pry out the two tiny fox-shaped ear bones, the *zorritos*, and play drinking games with them. A curandero also uses a live cuy diagnostically, rubbing it over a patient's body and reading the organs, "the X-ray of the Andes" ([earthstoriez](https://earthstoriez.com/cuy-guinea-pig-in-history-mythology-folklore-everyday-life-2)).

**Caldo de gallina and the soups.** Hen broth with noodles and potato is the dawn restorative and hangover cure ([Worldfood](https://worldfood.guide/dish/caldo-de-gallina/)). **Chairo** (Aymara for "soup") is the altiplano's flagship: chuño, dried lamb (chalona) or ch'arki, mote, favas, wheat, mint; thick farmer's fuel ([Chairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairo_(stew))). **Kapchi** of wild mushrooms appears only in the rains, December-March. Soup comes first at every meal; warmth and cheap calories at altitude, where hard work burns 3,250-4,600 kcal/day and the body prefers carbohydrate because it needs less oxygen per calorie ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK232869/)).

**Mote and the giant corn.** Mut'i is corn boiled with wood ash or lime and hulled; the Sacred Valley's paraqay sara (giant white Cusco corn) becomes choclo con queso, a fat cob with a slab of fresh cheese; hard chullpi corn is toasted into cancha, the universal table nibble ([Mote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mote_(food)); [Cuzco Eats](https://cuzcoeats.com/giant-white-corn-cuzcos-heart/)).

**Chicha de jora.** Corn is sprouted, dried, milled, boiled, and fermented days in a huge clay chomba; served still-fermenting, straw-colored, sour-cidery, only 1-3% alcohol, in half-liter caporal glasses. A **red flag or red plastic bag on a pole** over a doorway means "fresh chicha today." The brewers are women, chicheras, mother to daughter. First splash to the ground for Pachamama, always ([Chicha de jora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicha_de_jora); [Valencia Travel](https://www.valenciatravelcusco.com/passion-passport/what-is-a-chicheria)).

**Coca.** Leaves tucked between cheek and gum with a bite of **llipt'a** (compressed ash of quinoa stalks, like a charcoal briquette) that activates the alkaloids; it dulls hunger, cold, fatigue, and altitude, and numbs the cheek slightly. It is work fuel, clock, contract seal, and sacrament; see Section 4 ([Acullico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acullico); Allen 1981).

**Also on the table.** Olluco (waxy, stays crunchy, stewed with ch'arki), oca (tart fresh, sweet after days in the sun), mashua (peppery; famously fed to Inca soldiers to suppress libido, per Cobo 1653 and, oddly, modern rat studies), tarwi (lupin bean leached in running water for days), queso paria, alpaca meat (lean, faintly sweet), trout (introduced to Titicaca in 1941), pan chuta (a plate-sized sweet anise loaf from Oropesa's wood ovens, each bakery with its own mark), api (thick hot purple-corn breakfast drink), and t'anta wawa baby-shaped breads for All Souls.

**Meal etiquette.** Guests get a mountain of food; eating heartily is respect. Since you cannot finish it, wrapping leftovers in your carrying cloth to take home is correct and expected, not rude ([Allen](https://archive.org/details/holdlifehascoca00alle)). At communal meals, food is spread on woven cloths directly on the ground (the Quechua pampamesa, Aymara aptapi); eating in contact with the ground keeps the link to Pachamama ([Apthapi](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apthapi)). Soup by spoon; potatoes, mote, and cuy by hand. Men and elders are served first.

## 3. Quechua by Ear

Pronunciation notes: stress is almost always on the second-to-last syllable; *ll* = "ly," *ñ* = "ny"; apostrophes (k', t', ch') are little glottal "pops"; *q* is a swallowed back-of-throat k that colors nearby vowels toward "eh/oh."

1. **Allillanchu?** (ah-yee-YAHN-choo) — "Are you just fine?" The everyday greeting. Reply: **Allillanmi** (ah-yee-YAHN-mee), "just fine (I attest it)." Fuller: *Imaynalla kashanki? — Allillanmi, qanri?* ("How are you? Fine, and you?") ([UT-Austin Quechua Tinkuy](https://quechuatinkuy.coerll.utexas.edu/en/yachana-4/)).
2. **Sulpayki** (sool-PIE-kee) — everyday thanks; from *Diospagarasunki*, "may God repay you," worn smooth by centuries. Older campesinos still say the full form ([Omniglot](https://www.omniglot.com/language/phrases/quechua.php)).
3. **Urpillay sonqollay** (oor-PEE-lyai son-QO-lyai) — literally "my little dove, my little heart." A tender, song-like thank-you for something that moved you. *Yusulpayki, Urpillay*, "thank you, dear," is how Doña Juana accepts coca in Allen's ethnography (Allen 1981).
4. **Haku!** (HAH-koo) — "Let's go!" The most useful word in the language.
5. **Hamuy** (HAH-mooey) — "Come!" **Tiyay** (TEE-yai), "sit."
6. **Mamitay / Papay** (mah-MEE-tai / pah-PAI) — "my dear little mother / my father," said warmly to almost anyone. The engine is the suffix stack: -cha (little), -lla (dear/just), -y (my). *Sonqo* (heart) → *sonqollay*, "my dear little heart" ([El Comercio](https://elcomercio.pe/blog/cuscosparalelos/2015/11/nueve-expresiones-claves-en-el-diccionario-cusqueno/)).
7. **Wawa** (WAH-wah) — baby. **Wawqey / turay** — "my brother," but the word depends on the speaker's gender: a man says *wawqey*, a woman says *turay*; sister is *panay* (man speaking) or *ñañay* (woman speaking). Used for friends like "bro/sis." A great mechanic: the kin term reveals who is talking ([Quechua Tinkuy](https://quechuatinkuy.coerll.utexas.edu/en/yachana-4/)).
8. **Alalaw!** (ah-lah-LOW) — "How cold!"
9. **Akakaw!** — "How hot!"
10. **Achachaw!** — in Cusco-Collao, "How frightening!/Yikes!" (Many sources wrongly gloss it as cold or heat; the cold meaning is Ecuadorian Kichwa) ([Qheswa Simi Taqe](https://arqueologiadelperu.com/diccionario-quechua-espanol-quechua-qheswa-espanol-qheswa-simi-taqe/); [RAE](https://www.rae.es/tdhle/achachay)).
11. **Añañaw!** (ah-nyah-NYOW) — "How lovely!/How delicious!" Delight at something pretty or tasty.
12. **Atatay!** — "How disgusting!" **Ananaw!/Ayayaw!** — "Ouch!"
13. **Sumaq** (SOO-mahq) — beautiful AND delicious; one word for both. *Sumaq mikhuna*, wonderful food.
14. **Arí / Manan** — yes / no. **Yaw!** — "Hey!" (informal hail).
15. **Tupananchiskama** (too-pah-nan-chees-KAH-mah) — "until we meet again," the warm goodbye; **ratukama**, "see you in a bit."

Bonus texture, worth building into dialogue rather than glossaries. Quechua grammar forces speakers to mark how they know things: -mi (I saw it myself), -si (so they say), -chá (I'm guessing). Gossip and folktales run on -si; the habit bleeds into Andean Spanish as "dice que..." and sentence-final "pe," "nomás," "ya" ([Faller dissertation](https://personalpages.manchester.ac.uk/staff/martina.t.faller/documents/thesis-a4.pdf); [Andean Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_Spanish)). Flag for the writers' room: **"Ama suwa, ama llulla, ama qhella"** ("don't steal, don't lie, don't be lazy") is almost certainly NOT an authentic Inca greeting; it is a modern indigenist slogan absent from the early chroniclers. An elder proudly reciting it as "what the Inca taught" is realistic folklore; presenting it as ancient fact is an error ([elbuho.pe](https://elbuho.pe/2021/03/falso-yonhy-lescano-ama-quella-ama-sua-ama-llulla-son-los-postulados-sabios-de-los-incas/)).

## 4. Social Norms a Visitor Would Get Slightly Wrong

This section leans on Catherine Allen's fieldwork in Sonqo (70km from Cusco, 3,900m) ([Allen 1981](https://ipearqueologia.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/allen-quechua.pdf); [*The Hold Life Has*](https://archive.org/details/holdlifehascoca00alle)).

### Ayni and mink'a: how reciprocal labor actually works
- **Ayni** is symmetrical delayed exchange between equals: I work your potato harvest today, you owe me a day of equivalent work, and everyone keeps the ledger in their heads. "Life is an ayni; all activities are interwoven through this medium" (Núñez del Prado, quoted in Allen 1981). **Mink'a** is asymmetrical: services repaid in food, goods, or harvest share rather than returned labor. **Faena** is the obligatory communal workday (canal cleaning, school roof) called by the authorities, skippable only at the cost of a fine.
- **The request is sealed with coca, not a handshake.** "A request to do ayni is accompanied by a handful of coca; once the coca is accepted there can be no backing out of the contractual obligation" (Allen 1981). Requests to officials ride on gifts of trago the same way; acceptance or refusal of the gift IS the answer.
- The host of a work party owes the workers a proper midday meal, chicha, and coca breaks. Shorting the food is remembered.
- **Cash is not a neutral substitute.** You cannot pay someone else to do your ayni turn; the obligation is personal ([Ayni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayni)). Ethnographers describe the preference for ayni over wages as "a refusal to bypass human reciprocity": labor debt builds a relationship, cash closes one ([Ayni in the Global Village](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/mjcsl/3239521.0008.101?rgn=main%3Bview%3Dfulltext)). Game beat: a player who offers soles for harvest help gets a polite refusal and quiet offense; the correct move is to show up at the neighbor's harvest next week.
- Nuance so it isn't romanticized: wage day-labor (jornal) coexists with ayni everywhere now, and studies note ayni is leaned on hardest by the poorest households who cannot pay wages ([Wutich et al., "Ayni Real and Imagined"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319864445)). Villagers argue about which arrangement a given job deserves.

### Ch'allay and t'inka: feeding the earth before you drink
- Before drinking chicha or trago, you pour or sprinkle a little on the ground for Pachamama; this pouring is **ch'allay**. A second gesture, dipping fingers and **flicking drops into the air** toward the mountains, is the **t'inka** (from *t'inkay*, "to flick with the fingers"), directed to the Tirakuna, the sacred places (Allen, *The Hold Life Has*).
- It is done with the first pour of every bottle, by whoever is about to drink, murmuring "Pachamama, Santa Tierra" or naming the local apu. In some regions it is daily and nearly unconscious ([Persistence of Chicha](https://www.americanindianmagazine.org/story/persistence-chicha); [Pachamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pachamama)).
- The logic is not gratitude but reciprocity with a demanding partner. Skipping it invites bad luck; stumbling, losing a sheep, or getting caught in fog are read as signs the Earth-beings are annoyed with you (Allen 1981).
- Drinking itself is communal: one bottle, one small glass, passed in rounds; you fill, hand the bottle on, drink, and return the glass. The invitation is **"Tomakusunchis,"** "let us drink together" (Allen; [Bolin, *Rituals of Respect*](https://www.ubcpress.ca/rituals-of-respect)). Refusing your turn outright is a snub; the polite dodge is to drink a token sip.

### K'intu: the three coca leaves, exactly
From Allen's step-by-step account (Allen 1981):
1. Offering coca to a companion: a small handful with the phrase **"Hallpakuy, Mamay"** ("please chew, Mama") or, between equals, **"Hallpakusunchis"** ("let us chew together"). The receiver accepts **with both hands**; accepting with one hand is rude. Reply: "Yusulpayki, Urpillay."
2. Each person searches out three of their best leaves, dark, shiny, unbroken, stacks them one atop another shiny side up, and holds them between thumb and forefinger of the right hand. That is the **k'intu**.
3. Before chewing, the k'intu is waved in front of the mouth and **blown on gently** while the chewer murmurs the invocation; this is **phukuy**. The minimal correct form names three things: Mother Earth, the sacred places (the Tirakuna or a named peak), and one's own community.
4. K'intus are then exchanged: you make one and extend it to your companion (both hands on formal occasions), who thanks you, blows it, and chews it, while making one for you. In groups, offer to men before women and elders before juniors; unreciprocated k'intus are not held against you; "the score will even out in future hallpays."
5. Rules: never work during hallpay; never spit out the quid (extremely bad manners); dropped coca is swept up and burned in the fire, never trampled. Refusing coca in routine hallpay effectively declares you an outsider (Allen 1981).
6. Travelers blow k'intus to introduce themselves to the mountains of a district they are entering; a visitor is explicitly taught which peak to address.

### Greetings and the warm-up
- "Virtually all personal interactions are governed by highly stylized forms of etiquette; conversations are 'framed' by elaborate greetings, thank-yous, farewells, and expressions of mutual esteem" (Allen 1981). Jumping straight to business is not just brisk, it is illegible.
- The warm-up runs: greeting, health of the person, health of the family and animals, the weather and the crops, and only then the errand. On paths, you greet everyone you pass, even strangers ([Bolin, *Rituals of Respect*](https://www.ubcpress.ca/rituals-of-respect)).
- Any serious visit is accompanied by a small prestation: coca, bread, fruit, or a bottle. The gift opens the conversation; the errand comes after.

### Homes, food, gifts, and money
- You do not walk into a courtyard; you stand at the gate and call out, and are invited in ("Haykuykuy!", come in). Inside, you will be fed. **Accept the food.** It will be too much; eat visibly and appreciatively, and take the remainder home wrapped in a cloth; that is the designed outcome, not an affront (Allen; [Cultural Atlas](https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/peruvian-culture/peruvian-culture-etiquette)). A flat refusal of food or drink is a refusal of the relationship.
- Gifts are never really free; every gift opens or continues an exchange. A returned plate should not come back empty.
- Money is discussed obliquely between neighbors; haggling at market is normal and cheerful, but pressing cash into contexts that call for reciprocity (labor, hospitality, ritual) is the classic outsider error.
- **Compadrazgo** is the other great web: sponsoring a child's baptism or first haircut (rutuchikuy, around age one to two, where each guest snips a lock and gives a gift) makes you the parents' compadre/comadre for life, a bond often heavier than blood ([what-when-how](https://what-when-how.com/social-and-cultural-anthropology/compadrazgo-anthropology/)). A community office (cargo) is formally accepted or declined by accepting or declining the bundle of coca the mayor offers (Allen 1981).

## 5. Textiles and Dress

- **Lliclla**: a rectangular handwoven shoulder mantle (~90×110cm), pinned at the chest with a tupu (long silver pin) or nowadays a safety pin. The larger, sturdier **k'eperina** is knotted across the chest to carry a baby or a market load in a bundle called a **q'epi** ([Lliklla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lliklla); [Threads of Peru](https://threadsofperu.com/pages/traditional-andean-clothng)).
- **Chullo**: the earflap hat, and **men knit it**. On Taquile island boys learn to knit at five or six from their fathers; a boy's first chullo is white; red-and-white marks a single man, all-red a married one; and a prospective father-in-law tests a suitor by pouring water into his inverted chullo, tight knitting holds ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/taquile-and-its-textile-art-00166); [Andean Textile Arts](https://andeantextilearts.org/knitting-weaving-and-courtship-on-the-isle-of-taquile/)). The "tail position signals romantic availability" detail is soft tourist lore; use with a wink.
- **Pollera**: layered wool skirts of bayeta cloth, several for daily wear, ten or more for fiestas, trimmed with a golón border band. Wearing pollera is an assertion of Andean womanhood, not a costume ([LLILAS-Benson](https://sites.utexas.edu/llilas-benson-magazine/2017/08/28/making-beauty-the-wearing-of-polleras-in-the-andean-altiplano/)).
- **Montera**: the hat that names your village; an experienced eye places a woman's community by her hat and the beaded sanq'apa chin-strap. The bowler is a Bolivian/Aymara marker, not Peruvian sierra ([Threads of Peru](https://threadsofperu.com/pages/traditional-andean-clothng)).
- Also: the jobona (button-decorated jacket), ojotas (tire-tread sandals), and the chumpi belt, which supports the lower back under loads and swaddles babies.
- **Pallay** ("to pick up") is the finger-picked patterning: motifs held in memory, never written. Real documented motifs: inti (sun), ch'aska (star/Venus), ñawi (eye), mayu q'enqo (zigzag river / Milky Way), qocha (lake), ch'unchu (the jungle dancer; in Q'ero it stands for Inkarri), loraypu (Chinchero's signature flower), amaru (serpent), the colonial-era sirena. Colorways and borders genuinely identify communities; the idea that every symbol has one fixed secret meaning is overclaimed ([CTTC](https://www.textilescusco.org/community-detail/chinchero/6); [Threads of Peru](https://threadsofperu.com/pages/quechua-symbols-patterns)).
- **The backstrap loom in the courtyard**: warp stretched between two bars, one lashed to a post, the other on a strap around the weaver's lower back. She kneels, and tension IS her body: lean back to tighten, forward to open the shed. Tools: the illawa heddle stick and the **ruk'i / wichuna**, a polished llama bone pick. Weaving is social; a fine lliclla is weeks of work ([Threads of Peru](https://threadsofperu.com/pages/backstrap-weaving-in-peru); [Awamaki](https://www.awamaki.org/pages/techniques)).
- Gendered split: women spin (the pushka drop spindle, going constantly, even while walking) and weave the prestige pieces; men knit chullos and work the treadle loom for plain bayeta yardage. **Lloq'e**, yarn deliberately spun left-handed, is believed protective; worn by pregnant women and used in healing ([ClothRoads](https://www.clothroads.com/spinning-andean-stories-handspun-yarn/)).
- **Dyes**: cochineal insects scraped off prickly-pear pads (~150,000 insects per kilo of dye) give the reds through purples; q'olle flowers yellow, ch'illca leaves green, nogal walnut brown, a rock lichen orange (fixed with aged urine). Cheap synthetic anilinas nearly erased this; the revival (CTTC, founded 1996 by Nilda Callañaupa of Chinchero) rebuilt the recipes by interviewing elders ([Mosqoy](https://www.mosqoy.org/natural-dyes); [Andean Textile Arts](https://andeantextilearts.org/andean-natural-dyes-practical-deep-and-empowering/)).

## 6. Physical Texture of the Village

- **Houses**: adobe bricks molded in bottomless wooden forms (roughly 30-40cm long, mud tempered with chopped ichu straw), cured in the dry season, laid in mud mortar on a stone foundation. Small windows for warmth and earthquakes; tamped-earth floors. House-raising is done by ayni and paid in chicha, coca, and a meal ([Ecosur](http://english.ecosur.org/index.php/ecomaterials/earth-construction/41-building-with-earth-adobe-mud-bricks-compressed-soil); [World Housing](https://www.world-housing.net/wp-content/uploads/2011/06/Adobe_Blondet.pdf)).
- **Roofs**: ichu grass thatch (warm, needs near-annual renewal) giving way to calamina corrugated steel (cheap, durable, cold, loud in hail); the switch reads as money and modernity, a status signal NPCs can disagree about ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-5309/16/5/1032)). On the ridgeline, the **toritos de Pucará**: two ceramic bulls flanking a cross ([PeruRail](https://www.perurail.com/blog-en/fall-in-love-with-the-symbolism-and-art-of-torito-de-pucara/)).
- **The compound (kancha)**: separate one-room buildings (sleeping room, kitchen, store) around a walled patio with one gate. The patio is the workshop: loom staked out, chuño drying, potatoes sorted, chickens underfoot.
- **The kitchen**: a q'oncha stove of stones and mud burning eucalyptus and dung, usually no chimney, so walls are glossy black with soot; sheepskins on low benches; guinea pigs whistling in the woodpile ([Cuzco Eats](http://cuzcoeats.com/the-andean-kitchen-a-special-traditional-space/)).
- **A day of weather at 3,800m**: frost at dawn; fast warming under violent sun (Cusco's UV index averages "extreme"); 15-18°C by midday; afternoon cloud build-up, in the rainy season a convective storm with possible hail; then a temperature crash at sunset. The diurnal swing runs 20-25°C ([WeatherSpark Puno](https://weatherspark.com/y/26593/Average-Weather-in-Puno-Peru-Year-Round)).
- **The night sky**: the Milky Way is **Mayu**, the river, and people read the dark dust lanes, not the stars: Yacana the llama, her baby, Atoq the fox, Yutu the tinamou; their risings are an agricultural calendar ([Astronoo](https://astronoo.com/en/andean-astronomy.html)).
- **Smells**: eucalyptus and dung smoke at cooking hours, wet wool, corral, fermenting chicha, boiling chuño soup, muña crushed underfoot, wet earth after rain. **Sounds**: roosters, dogs all night, constant wind, the beat of the loom, the whir of a spindle, a herder's whistle, Quechua radio, church bells, guinea pigs.
- **Water**: springs (puquios), public standpipes, or the stream; managed by the community water committee; carried in buckets by women and children ([MDPI Water](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/13/19/2650)).
- **Layout**: plaza with colonial church; school; the community hall where the asamblea meets; the posta de salud with one nurse; a tienda or two; a chichería under its red flag; the cemetery at the edge; everything strung on one dirt road, with houses dispersing kilometers up toward the pastures ([Bolin](https://ebin.pub/growing-up-in-a-culture-of-respect-child-rearing-in-highland-peru-9780292795976.html)).

## 7. Tensions and Change (so NPCs can disagree)

- **Migration**: rural Peru loses ~2%/year; in one Puno community 81.6% of surveyed people had migrated. Teens leave for secondary school and don't return. Lima migrants ("residentes") bankroll the patron-saint fiesta from afar and return for it, which breeds a specific resentment between the cousin who sends money and the cousin who stayed and did the faenas ([arXiv case study](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03341)).
- **Language**: 3.8M Quechua speakers (2017 census), but 4.7M self-identify as Quechua; identity outlives the language. Parents stopped transmitting it out of stigma; meanwhile the young re-learn it from TikTok (Solischa, 330k followers) and Renata Flores's Quechua trap. The grandmother who hid her Quechua "to spare them" versus the grandchild re-learning it is a true, common conflict ([SciELO](http://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S2071-50722019000200002); [Ojo Público](https://ojo-publico.com/3326/dia-la-lengua-materna-defensa-del-quechua-y-el-kukama)).
- **Phones**: ~88% rural cell coverage but only ~22% home internet; life runs on WhatsApp and TikTok over mobile data, and on the specific hill where you get a bar ([INEI](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/inei/noticias/1133448-el-58-4-de-los-hogares-del-pais-tiene-acceso-a-internet)).
- **Tourism**: dependency cuts both ways; city operators capture the margin; the celebrated community-run Taquile model broke when Puno operators muscled in; weaving co-ops are both lifeline and site of old grievances ([Ojo Público](https://ojo-publico.com/2144/sobrevivir-sin-turismo-las-comunidades-cusco)).
- **Mining**: Las Bambas (200-300 ore trucks a day on a road that used to be communal), Espinar's heavy-metals grievance, La Rinconada's cachorreo system. Key realism: one household can contain a son doing mine shifts AND a family blockading the mine road, with no felt contradiction ([CooperAcción](https://cooperaccion.org.pe/opinion/las-bambas-cronica-de-un-conflicto-anunciado-que-parece-no-tener-fin/); [ILO](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/cachorreo-system-used-exploit-miners-la-rinconada)).
- **Climate and land**: Peru lost ~half its glacier surface in 40 years; potato cultivation has climbed 1,000m in 30 years and is running out of mountain; plots fragment with each inheritance ([IPS](https://ipsnoticias.net/2014/12/el-clima-amenaza-a-la-papa-y-a-los-quechuas-en-andes-peruanos/); [ANA](https://www.ana.gob.pe/noticia/peru-perdio-el-51-de-sus-glaciares-debido-al-cambio-climatico)).
- **Religion, the underrated fault line**: rural evangelicalism has grown to ~19%; converts refuse chicha, coca, Pachamama libations, and fiesta cargo sponsorship, which the Catholic majority reads as withdrawing from the community itself ([SciELO Chile](https://scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-10432016005000029&script=sci_arttext)).
- **Memory and politics**: the 1980-2000 internal conflict killed ~69,000; 75% of victims were Quechua-speakers, and it is living memory. The 2022-23 protests and the Juliaca massacre shape how the southern sierra sees Lima right now. Handle factually and with care ([CVR via RPP](https://rpp.pe/peru/actualidad/grafico-que-fue-la-cvr-y-que-dijo-su-informe-final-noticia-990203); [HRW](https://www.hrw.org/es/news/2023/05/10/peru-las-pruebas-refutan-la-version-oficial-sobre-las-muertes-en-juliaca)).
- **Gender**: ~97% of community presidencies are male; a 2021 law mandates 30% women on boards; a woman who insists on speaking at the assembly is a realistic, quietly radical character. The Fujimori-era forced sterilizations remain an unresolved wound for women now in their 50s-70s ([RPP](https://rpp.pe/politica/estado/al-menos-el-30-de-los-puestos-directivos-en-comunidades-campesinas-seran-ocupados-por-mujeres-noticia-1209667); [DEMUS](https://www.demus.org.pe/estaprobado-gobierno-de-fujimori-esterilizo-a-211-000-mujeres-sin-brindarles-informacion-completa/)).

## 8. Traps to Avoid

1. **No ayahuasca in the sierra.** Ayahuasca is Amazonian. The Andean plant is San Pedro/wachuma, and even that belongs to specific ritual contexts, not village daily life.
2. **They are not "Incas."** The Inca were a ruling state that fell in 1572. Many villagers self-identify first as campesinos, comuneros, or runa ([Americanía](https://www.upo.es/revistas/index.php/americania/article/download/5201/4548)).
3. **The Q'ero-mystic industry.** "Last pure Inca shamans" marketing de-historicizes a real, market-integrated community. No wise-shaman-dispenses-quest-wisdom archetype ([Marquette](https://epublications.marquette.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1007&context=jgecp)).
4. **Pachamama is not a gentle Gaia.** The relationship is reciprocal, demanding, and sometimes dangerous; offerings are obligations, not gratitude cards (De la Cadena, *Earth Beings*; Allen).
5. **Coca is not cocaine.** The conflation is offensive and illiterate; the leaf is legal, sacred, medicinal, and the medium of social life.
6. **Llamas carry; alpacas do not.** Alpacas are fiber animals. Vicuñas are wild and protected. "Loading the alpaca" is an instant tell.
7. **Bowler hats read as Bolivia.** Peruvian sierra women wear community-specific monteras. Also: real daily wear mixes polleras with tracksuits, sweaters, tire sandals, and phones.
8. **Kill the pan-flute soundtrack.** "El Cóndor Pasa" easy-listening is the sonic cliché. Real village audio: huayno (charango, quena, harp, high female vocals), sikuri ensembles in Puno, and, from the teenagers' phones, cumbia sureña and reggaetón ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/the-picture-show/2025/10/10/g-s1-92885/peru-chicha-cumbia-el-pulso-electrico-de-lima)).
9. **No timeless village.** Highland communities have been market-integrated for centuries and today have cash transfers, migration, smartphones, and national politics. Equally avoid poverty porn and "poor but happy."
10. **No white savior, no Limeño savior.** *Madeinusa* (2006) is the canonical cautionary tale. The praised counter-examples are insider-made: *Wiñaypacha*, *Willaq Pirqa*, *Retablo*, and in prose Arguedas and Pablo Landeo ([Wayka](https://wayka.pe/los-andes-de-winaypacha-por-monica-delgado/)).
11. **Language errors**: don't machine-translate Quechua; match the dialect and pick one vowel orthography; "Ama suwa, ama llulla, ama qhella" is a modern slogan, not an Inca greeting; achachaw means fright in Cusco, not cold.
12. **Names carry class.** Quechua surnames (Quispe, Mamani, Condori, Huamán, Apaza) are the most common in the sierra and carry real stigma in the cities. A believable village mixes them with Spanish surnames from old hacienda patrones ([La República](https://larepublica.pe/datos-lr/respuestas/2022/08/19/quispe-ochoa-y-otros-apellidos-de-origen-quechua-que-habrian-sido-cambiados-por-los-espanoles-peru-mestizaje-virreinato-conquista-del-peru-historia-incas-evat)).
13. **Puno is heavily Aymara.** Don't make the whole altiplano generically Quechua.
14. **Don't turn pallay into a decoder ring.** Motifs mark community and personal story; they are not a fixed symbol dictionary, and weavers say so themselves.

## Caveats for the writers' room

The 4 a.m. wake and the coldest nights describe the highest herding communities; a 3,500-3,800m farming village runs milder and wakes at 5-6. Section 4's k'intu/hallpay procedures are Allen's Paucartambo etiquette; she notes regional variation, so treat them as this village's specific way, not a pan-Andean law. Claims flagged inline as legend or tourist lore (bowler-hat origin, chullo tail-position code, motif "decoder ring," the Inca triple greeting) are safe as things characters say, unsafe as things the game asserts. Strongest next step for more depth: physical copies of Allen's *The Hold Life Has*, Bolin's two books, and Nilda Callañaupa's weaving books.
