# Elsewhere: Sicily Chapter Content Bible

**Setting:** a fictional small fishing town on Sicily's east coast, in the Aci Trezza / Riviera dei Ciclopi tradition, under Etna. The player arrives by ship from Zanzibar via the Suez Canal, which is the historically correct steamship route into the Mediterranean. Fictional town, real texture: everything below is drawn from the real culture of this coast.

## 1. The Passeggiata

The evening walk is the chapter's spine and the game's thesis made literal. Between roughly 5 and 8 pm the whole town dresses a little nicer and strolls the lungomare and piazza with no destination at all. Sociologists call it a cultural performance; it is people watching, flirting, gossip, nodding to strangers, admiring newborns, and arguing about football, all at walking pace. Historically it was also courtship display, families showing off marriageable daughters. Whole families walk together on Sundays. Design note: this is the direct rhyme with the Peru chapter's promenade. The rule the player learns: the walk has no goal, and that is the point. People matter more than things.

## 2. Fish Market Theater

Model on the Catania pescheria behind Piazza del Duomo, mornings only, roughly 7 am to 1 pm. The signature is the **abbanniata**, the sung vendor's cry: each stall has its own melody, pitch, and rhythm, passed down within families, so the market becomes an operatic, polyphonic soundscape. Swordfish heads stare from the ice; knives flourish over whole tuna; haggling is performance with an audience. This rhymes with Peru's end-of-day remate: commerce as theater. Background lore, mentioned by elders rather than shown: the **tonnara**, the fixed tuna-trap fishery of Arab-taught technique practiced for over a millennium, and the mattanza, its ritual killing of the catch. The tonnare died out by the late twentieth century from collapsing stocks; an old man in the town can remember one. (Soft claim: the famous tonnare are mostly western Sicily, e.g. Favignana; east-coast memory of them should stay secondhand.)

## 3. Aci Trezza Specifics

- **The faraglioni.** Basalt sea stacks just offshore, called the Islands of the Cyclops. Legend, via the Odyssey: blinded Polyphemus hurled these boulders at Ulysses' escaping ships. The Cyclopes were said to keep their forge under Etna. The town lives facing its own myth.
- **Verga's I Malavoglia (1881).** Aci Trezza is the setting of the founding novel of Italian verismo: the Malavoglia family, the house by the medlar tree (casa del Nespolo), and their boat **la Provvidenza**, which sinks in a storm carrying a cargo of lupini beans, killing Bastianazzo and ruining the family. The real Aci Trezza has a Casa del Nespolo museum, with a room for Visconti's 1948 film adaptation *La Terra Trema*. Design note: the town IS Italian literature's fishing village. Name a boat Provvidenza; let a character quote the novel's fatalism about leaving and returning.
- **Festival: San Giovanni Battista, June 24.** Verified: the pantomime **U pisci a mari** ("the fish in the sea"), dating to around 1750. Four rowers in a decorated boat, directed by a chief fisherman (the rais), repeatedly try to catch the swordfish, played by a strong swimmer who keeps escaping. It is a propitiatory rite parodying the old swordfish hunt of the Strait of Messina. Direct rhyme with Peru's San Pedrito: two towns, two saints, both of whom go down to the sea.

## 4. Food, With Rules

Food here has grammar, and breaking it is characterization.

- **Granita con brioche** is breakfast, not dessert. Flavors: lemon, almond (mandorla), pistachio, coffee, mulberry (gelsi, a short June-July season), strawberry, peach. The **brioche col tuppo** has a topknot named for the chignon of Sicilian women; you tear off the tuppo first and dip it.
- **Arancino vs arancina.** The war of names. Catania and the east say arancino (masculine, often cone-shaped, and locals say the cone honors Etna); Palermo and the west say arancina (feminine, spherical, like the orange it is named for). Both sides argue from grammar. In this town it is arancino, and correcting a visitor is a small ritual of belonging.
- **Pasta alla Norma.** Catania's dish: fried eggplant, tomato, basil, grated ricotta salata, named for Bellini's opera Norma. Bellini was Catanese; the naming legend has a delighted diner exclaiming the dish was "a true Norma," meaning a masterpiece. Local pride runs through it.
- **Cannolo.** Filled at the moment of ordering, never before, or the shell goes soggy. A pre-filled cannolo is an insult the player can learn to spot.
- Also in rotation: **caponata** (sweet-sour eggplant), **pane cunzato** ("seasoned bread," the poor man's bread of oil, tomato, cheese, anchovy, oregano), **latte di mandorla**, and the lemon and citrus groves running down to the lava.

## 5. The Sunday Pranzo

Sunday lunch is sacred, multigenerational time; it starts at lunch and can last until evening. Shape: antipasto, primo (in the south often pasta al forno or a Sicilian ragù), secondo, then fruit, dolce, coffee. The nonna orchestrates; recipes pass by watching her hands, not by cookbook. Refusing a second helping requires diplomacy. On hospitality, use verified proverbs (section 6) rather than invented ones. (Soft claim: the specific saying "cu veni a me casa" could not be verified in sources; do not use it verbatim. The hospitality ethic itself is well attested.)

## 6. Sicilian by Ear

Sicilian is a language coexisting with Italian, not a comedy accent. Characters slide between the two: Italian for officialdom and strangers, Sicilian for feeling. Ear-words for the player: **amunì** (come on, let's go), **bedda/beddu** (beautiful/handsome, also an endearment), **picciriddu/picciridda** (little kid), **talìa!** (look!), **camurrìa** (a nuisance, a pain). Verified proverbs:

- *Cu nesci arrinesci.* "Who leaves, succeeds." The emigration proverb; it should sting in a town of departures, and it speaks to the traveling player.
- *Cu mancia fa muddichi.* "Who eats makes crumbs." Everything you do leaves traces.
- *Cumu veni si cunta.* Roughly "we'll deal with it as it comes."

## 7. Scopa at the Circolo

The **circolo** (social club; here, a circolo dei pescatori) is the institution: same old men, same table, cards, smoke, and lifetime membership. Scopa minigame sketch, verified rules: 40-card Sicilian deck. Deal 3 cards each, 4 face up on the table. Play one card per turn to capture a table card of equal value, or a combination summing to it; otherwise your card stays on the table. Clearing the table entirely is a **scopa** (a "sweep," worth a point, marked by a face-up sideways card). End-of-round points: most cards, most coins (denari), the settebello (seven of coins), and best primiera (built on sevens). First to 11 wins. Briscola exists at the next table for flavor.

## 8. Etna as Neighbor

Locals say **'a Muntagna**, "the Mountain," with the feminine article: she is a presence, half feared, half loved, the rhyme with Peru's la mar. Ash on cars and balconies after an eruption is swept off with a shrug; she erupts multiple times a year and the sky glows red. The town is built of her: lava-stone doorways, black cobbles, baroque churches in grey and black basalt. On her slopes, vineyard terraces held by dry lava-stone walls produce mineral Etna wines. Catania's own name likely comes from Greek for "at the foot of Etna."

## 9. Texture Sheet

Lava-black shoreline against lemon groves; laundry strung between balconies; Vespas threading alleys; church bells marking hours; beaded door curtains clicking in doorways (soft claim: ubiquitous in southern Italian towns, thinly documented as specifically Sicilian); painted wooden boats hauled up on black rock; nets drying; and in August the **Ferragosto** feeling: the whole country at the beach on the 15th, bonfires and midnight swims on the night of the 14th, grilled lunches with relatives, fireworks over the water.

## 10. Traps (Zero Tolerance)

- **No mafia.** No Godfather nods, no winking "family" jokes, no coppola-and-shotgun iconography. This is the trap the whole chapter must not fall into; the town's drama is the sea, money, and leaving, exactly as in Verga.
- **No mainland bleed.** This is Sicily, not generic Italy. No gondolas, no Tuscan sunflowers, no Roman gestures presented as local.
- **No pizza-centrism.** Pizza is Naples. Here it is arancini, pasta alla Norma, granita, fish.
- **Dialect is not comedy.** Sicilian is a language of intimacy and precision, never a punchline.
