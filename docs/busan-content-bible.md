# Elsewhere: Busan Chapter Content Bible (Short Chapter)

**Fictional neighborhood:** Mulmang-gol, a harborside market quarter folded between the fish quay and a hillside of pastel stairs. Real texture, invented streets. Modeled on Nampo-dong, Jagalchi, and the hillside villages, but never named as any of them.

## Arrival: the night ferry

The player crosses from Shimonoseki, Japan on the real overnight ferry. The Busan-Shimonoseki route opened in 1905 under Japanese rule, was suspended in 1945, and was revived in June 1970 after Korea-Japan normalization. Today two ships run it: the Japanese Kanpu Ferry's Hamayu and the Korean Pukwan Ferry's Seonghee. Depart around 9 pm, arrive the next morning; the ship actually reaches port before dawn and waits at anchor until immigration opens around 8 am. Game beat: the player wakes to gulls and container cranes, forced to be slow. The ferry itself carries a century of complicated history; we acknowledge it in one quiet line from an elder passenger, no more.

## The market: women run the fish chain

Anchor location, fictionalized from Jagalchi, Korea's largest seafood market. The load-bearing fact: after the Korean War, when Busan was the temporary capital swollen with refugees and many men were dead or gone, women took over the fish trade. The "Jagalchi ajumma" (market aunties), many of them war widows and refugees, built the market with basins and squid knives, and today many successful stalls are run by their descendants. This rhymes directly with Marisol in Peru; the player should notice it themselves. Our vendor matriarch, working name **Halmae Sun-ok**, is dignity first: weathered hands, rubber apron, terrifying arithmetic, sudden tenderness. Never comic relief.

Market geography from the real thing: live fish tanks and shellfish on one side, a dried seafood alley on the other (dried squid, anchovies, the smell that clings to your coat). Dawn auctions between roughly 5 and 9 am, chanted numbers, crates of ice. The upstairs ritual: pick your fish from a tank downstairs, carry it up, and a restaurant prepares it as hoe (Korean raw fish), grilled, or as soup. Great quest structure: choose, climb, share.

## The word: jeong, and the gesture: deom

**Jeong (정)** is the chapter's untranslatable concept. Not love, not friendship: a bond that accumulates through shared time, food, and small acts, often unnamed until tested. Koreans describe it as forming between people, but also with places and objects. It has thorny variants (miun jeong, "hateful affection," the jeong you develop even for someone who irritates you), which is good material for the player's relationship with a brusque vendor.

**Deom (덤)** is the chapter's answer to the yapa, and it is nearly exact: at Korean markets a vendor throws in a little extra, a few more strawberries, one more fish cake, unannounced. Related is "service" (seobiseu), the free dish or drink a restaurant owner brings out for a regular. The deom is jeong made visible; the vendor is saying the relationship matters more than the margin. Sun-ok gives the player a deom before they know the word. The reveal lands later, in the teahouse.

**"Bap meogeosseo?" (Have you eaten?)** is the everyday greeting of care, a hello, a worry, and a love letter in three words, rooted in eras when being fed meant being well. Use it as the neighborhood's recurring greeting; by chapter's end the player asks it first.

## Banchan: the table that fills itself

Korean meals arrive with banchan, small free side dishes, refilled without charge. Roots run to Buddhist and Confucian traditions, but the free-refill generosity hardened during postwar scarcity, when vegetables were cheaper than rice and eateries made sure people left full; diners took to calling restaurant women "emo" (auntie). Scene: the player orders one humble thing and watches the table crowd with little plates. This is a second, softer rhyme with the yapa: abundance as default hospitality, not transaction.

## Food, eaten standing or crouched

- **Ssiat hotteok:** Busan's signature seed hotteok, a fried brown-sugar pancake split and stuffed with sunflower and pumpkin seeds and nuts. Local pride vs. the plain Seoul version.
- **Eomuk:** fish cake skewers standing in salty broth; you sip the broth free from a shared kettle. Busan makes Korea's most famous eomuk.
- **Dwaeji gukbap:** Busan's milky pork and rice soup, whole alleys of it, a dish with refugee-era roots. [soft: origin stories vary; keep the history one line]
- **Tteokbokki and sikhye:** spicy rice cakes; sweet rice punch as the non-alcoholic finisher.
- **Pojangmacha:** orange tent bars glowing at night, steam, low stools, laughter. Texture only; the player shares eomuk broth and sikhye, never needs to drink.

## The teahouse and the sage

A quiet traditional teahouse up the stairs from the market. Korean tea practice, darye, literally "tea etiquette," carries a thousand years of Buddhist and Confucian slowness; the humbler dabang tradition served ssanghwacha, a dark sweet herbal tea. Our elder, working name **Teacher Mun**, serves ssanghwacha with a floating egg yolk [soft: old dabang custom, verify before shipping] and speaks almost entirely in questions. Her planted riddle, carried for chapters: **"When the auntie gives you one fish more than you paid for, who is in debt?"** The eventual answer, no one and everyone, is ayni, yapa, and deom in one sentence.

## Language by ear

Annyeonghaseyo (hello), gamsahamnida (thank you), **jal meokgetseumnida** ("I will eat well," said before eating) and **jal meogeotseumnida** ("I ate well," after), a paired before/after mechanic. Eomma (mom), ajumma/ajeossi (middle-aged woman/man; respectful when sincere, rude when careless, teach the player the difference). **Ppalli-ppalli** ("hurry hurry"): the speed ethic of postwar reconstruction that built modern Korea, and the chapter's friction against the game's walk-slowly thesis. Busan's counterweight: the market moves ppalli-ppalli at dawn and then sits down to a long lunch.

## Etiquette friction beats

1. Receive anything with two hands, or right hand supported by the left.
2. Never stand chopsticks upright in rice (funeral incense).
3. Pour for elders first, two hands on the bottle; turn slightly away when drinking before an elder (broth counts for our purposes).
4. Shoes off at the teahouse floor seating.

## Texture list

Gulls trailing the ferry; container cranes like orange giraffes; hillside villages of stacked pastel houses (real model: Gamcheon, built by wartime refugees including the Taegukdo religious community, painted by artists from 2009); noraebang (karaoke room) muffled through walls at night; the ammonia-sweet smell of the dried squid alley; auction chants; broth steam under tent light.

## Traps

- Do not write Seoul with a harbor. Busan is saltier, blunter, its dialect (satoori) rougher and warmer.
- No K-pop or K-drama references. This neighborhood predates the wave and ignores it.
- War history stays in the ground it built (the market, the hillside), one or two quiet lines, never a lecture.
- The ajumma is the backbone of the economy, not a haggling cartoon. Loud, yes; a joke, never.

## Soft claims flagged

Egg-yolk ssanghwacha in dabang; precise dwaeji gukbap origins; "widows founded Jagalchi" is directionally solid but part oral tradition, so let characters say it rather than narration asserting it.
