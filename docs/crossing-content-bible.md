# The Crossing: Content Bible
### Elsewhere, Chapter: Callao to Yokohama

Premise: the player works passage in the galley of a fictional mid-size container ship on a roughly 30-day transpacific run from Callao, Peru toward Japan. The ship is a village of about two dozen people; the crew is the culture; the sky is the throughline.

## 1. Life Aboard

- **Route reality**: Callao to Japan is about 9,366 nautical miles; real container services quote roughly 31 to 32 days port to port, with sailings every one to two weeks. A 25 to 30 day pure sea passage is credible for the game.
- **Crew size**: automation cut crews from 40 to 50 in the 1980s to roughly 20 to 25 today: master, three or four deck officers, chief engineer plus three engineers, bosun, three or four able seamen (ABs), motorman, a chief cook, and one or two messmen or stewards.
- **Watches**: deck officers stand two four-hour watches per day, "four on, eight off": 12 to 4 (the midwatch), 4 to 8, 8 to 12. Ship's bells mark each half hour; eight bells ends a watch ("eight bells and all's well"). The bell is mostly ceremonial now, but the watch grid still rules everyone's sleep.
- **Galley rhythm**: three fixed meals plus night lunch for watchkeepers. The chief cook runs the galley with a messman assisting; provisioning is budgeted as a daily victualling rate per head (exact modern figures vary by company; keep the number vague in-game). Fresh produce fades week by week; the freezer and the rice sack take over. The cook is quietly one of the most important people aboard, and crews call the mess room the heart of the ship.
- **Recreation**: karaoke machines, movie nights, ping-pong, video games, basketball hoops rigged in a hold or on deck, Sunday barbecues (documented morale staples per the Seattle Times and Hapag-Lloyd). Internet was historically slow and rationed; Starlink-era ships often have real Wi-Fi, which some say emptied the mess room. Pick one era and commit; a patchy-internet ship makes better drama.
- **Contracts**: Filipino ratings commonly serve 6 to 12 months, often around 9; European officers frequently do 3 to 4. Homesickness is structural, not incidental.
- **Time**: sailing west, clocks retard one hour every day or two; the classic fair split gives each of the three watches 20 minutes of the extra hour. A westbound ship crosses the International Date Line and skips a calendar day entirely. Great quest hook: someone's birthday vanishes.

## 2. Crew Demographics and Texture

- **The numbers**: Filipinos are about 25 percent of the world's roughly 1.6 million seafarers and crew close to 30 percent of the merchant fleet; the Philippines is the top supplier nation. Seafarer remittances were about $6.7 billion in 2022; total OFW remittances top $34 billion a year. Other major supply nations: India, China, Indonesia, Russia, Ukraine, Poland, Myanmar. European or Indian officers over Filipino and mixed-Asian ratings is a common pattern, not a rule.
- **Design the crew mixed**: e.g., a Croatian captain, an Indian chief engineer, a Ukrainian second officer, Filipino ABs and cook, an Indonesian oiler, a Chinese electrician. Give each a hometown, a contract clock, and one thing they miss.
- **Galley Tagalog/Taglish** (common phrases, standard usage): "Kain na!" (come and eat, the great summons), "Kumusta?" (how are you), "pare"/"pre" (buddy), "salamat" (thank you), "masarap!" (delicious), "gutom na ako" (I'm hungry), "ingat" (take care), "tara" (let's go), "sige" (okay). Taglish, the fluid mix of Tagalog and English, is the realistic register; shipboard slang also borrows Hindi terms on mixed crews (soft claim).
- **Homesickness dishes**: chicken or pork adobo (soy, vinegar, garlic; the dish OFWs learn first abroad), sinigang (sour tamarind soup), pancit (noodles; long life, birthdays). Cooking lutong bahay (home cooking) aboard is described by seafarers as medicine for homesickness. A quest sourcing calamansi or tamarind substitutes in Callao writes itself.
- **Karaoke** is sacred and sincere; ballads get full commitment, and videoke scoring machines are common. Ritual, not comedy.

## 3. The Line-Crossing Ceremony

- Callao sits south of the equator, so a route toward Japan honestly crosses the line mid-ocean.
- **History**: at least 400 years old in Western seafaring. Uninitiated sailors are pollywogs; the initiated are shellbacks, Sons and Daughters of Neptune. The court: Neptunus Rex, Queen Amphitrite, Davy Jones, a royal barber, a royal baby, royal bears who dunk pollywogs in brine.
- **Modern practice**: after hazing scandals, navies made it voluntary theatre; merchant ships run a short, kind, officer-led version: mock summons, silly charges ("presuming to cross the line without King Neptune's consent"), flour-and-slop grooming, a saltwater dousing, a certificate signed by the master and treasured for life. The game version should be gentle, consent-based, and end with the cook's feast.
- Related honor: crossing the date line earns the Domain of the Golden Dragon (a US naval tradition; soft claim for merchant ships, but a fun certificate to invent).

## 4. One Sky, Many Readings (Chapter Theme)

- **Quechua/Andean**: the Milky Way is Mayu, the river; sky water returns to earth as rain. The signature idea is dark cloud constellations (yana phuyu): animal silhouettes in the dust lanes. Yacana the llama (with a suckling baby, near the Southern Cross and Scorpius, best November to April) descends nightly to drink the rivers and prevent floods. Others: Yutu the tinamou, Hanp'atu the toad, Machacuay the serpent.
- **Polynesian wayfinding**: the Hawaiian star compass (Nainoa Thompson, Hokule'a) divides the horizon into 32 houses of 11.25 degrees; star lines include Ke Ka o Makali'i (the Canoe-Bailer), Ka Iwikuamo'o (the Backbone), Manaiakalani (the Chief's Fishline), Ka Lupe o Kawelo (the Kite). Hokule'a is Arcturus; Hokupa'a is Polaris. A crew member who knows this art should be its student, not a mystical sage.
- **Japanese**: the Milky Way is Amanogawa, the River of Heaven, separating the weaver Orihime (Vega) and the cowherd Hikoboshi (Altair), who meet once a year on the seventh day of the seventh month: Tanabata, when wishes are tied to bamboo. Arriving in Japan near July 7 gives the chapter its landing beat.
- **The officers' sky**: sextants are still taught and carried as GPS backup; a mate teaching the player a star sight bridges the traditions.
- Theme line: five people on the dark bridge wing, one river of stars, four names for it.

## 5. Working Passage: The Truth

- Working for passage is essentially extinct today; manning regulations, insurance, visas, and the MLC make it impractical. Real freighter travel is paid passage, roughly $100 to $150 per day, 2 to 12 passengers, booked months ahead. Historically, cargo liners routinely carried workers and passengers together. Our galley-hand premise is a knowing fiction; keep the surrounding details true and consider one wry line acknowledging the captain "doing it the old way."

## 6. Gold: Details and Set Pieces

- **Superstitions across cultures**: no whistling (it whistles up a storm); no bananas aboard (fishermen's taboo); never harm an albatross, which carries sailors' souls (Coleridge); a ship's cat as mascot and weather oracle (fur licked against the grain means storm coming). Let each nationality contribute one superstition and argue about which are real.
- **Night texture**: permanent engine hum and hull vibration, red-lit bridge, flying fish stranded on deck at dawn (the cook can fry them), an albatross holding station off the stern for days, phosphorescence in the wake.
- **The horn**: in fog, one prolonged blast (4 to 6 seconds) every two minutes underway; two prolonged if stopped (COLREGS Rule 35). A foghorn day is a mood.
- **Garbage**: MARPOL Annex V forbids throwing plastics overboard; the garbage log is real bureaucracy a rookie could bungle.

## Traps to Avoid

- No all-Filipino monolith: individuals with different provinces, languages (Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano), ranks, and ambitions.
- Don't romanticize the labor: fatigue, hierarchy, and missed funerals are real; keep warmth without erasing cost.
- No pirate cliches, parrots, or "arr"; no mystical-native navigator trope; karaoke played straight, never as mockery.
- Officers-white, ratings-brown is a pattern to complicate, not a law to reproduce.
