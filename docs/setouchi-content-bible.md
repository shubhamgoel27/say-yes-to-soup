# Elsewhere Content Bible: Setouchi Chapter
**Fictional town: Shionoura (塩之浦), a small fishing town on Japan's Seto Inland Sea. Player arrives by cargo ship from Peru in early July.**

Fictional town, real texture. Every detail below is grounded in real Setouchi sources; soft or invented claims are flagged.

## 1. The Town and Its Rhythm

The Seto Inland Sea holds over 700 islands, calm water threaded by small ferries and shipping lanes; historically it was Japan's main trade artery. Shionoura is a composite of real places like Tomonoura, Ushimado, and Setoda: sheltered port, stone lantern at the harbor mouth, houses stacked up a slope of citrus terraces.

**Morning rhythm.** Boats go out before dawn; the small port market moves fast and early. The pride catch is **tai (red sea bream)**, fished here since the Edo period; Tomonoura's **tai-ami** net fishery is roughly 380 years old. Households eat the day's catch simply: sashimi, grilled fish, rice, miso soup, with **tai-somen** (sea bream over thin noodles) for celebrations. (Soft claim: the exact "fisherman's breakfast" plate is a plausible composite, not a documented fixed menu.)

**The shotengai.** A half-covered shopping street: fishmonger, tofu shop, a shuttered storefront or two (honest detail for a shrinking town), **noren** curtains hung in doorways to say "we're open." The police presence at this scale is realistically a **chūzaisho** (residential police post, officer lives there with family) rather than an urban kōban; the chūzaisho officer who knows everyone is the truer character.

**Demographics.** Like most Inland Sea islands, Shionoura is elderly-majority in feel. On Naoshima, residents 65+ are about 35 percent versus roughly 28 percent nationally; many smaller islands skew far older. Kids are few, the school is small, the ferry timetable structures everything.

**The Naoshima effect (gentle modern tension).** Since the 1990s, Benesse's art projects and the Setouchi Triennale have drawn huge visitor numbers to depopulating islands (Naoshima: about 36,000 visitors in 1992, over 720,000 in 2016). In-game: a young returnee wants to open a gallery cafe in a vacant house; an old fisherman is skeptical but quietly proud that outsiders find his sea beautiful. Warm, not polemical.

## 2. Omiyage: The Chapter's Core Mechanic

**Omiyage** are gifts a traveler brings back for the people who stayed behind. Unlike a souvenir, an omiyage is not for yourself; it is typically a regional food in small individually wrapped portions, easy to distribute. It carries real social weight (skipping it can be a small breach; it thanks those who covered for you while you were away) but the joy is real too: giving omiyage is how a trip is shared.

**Meibutsu** means "famous thing": every place in Japan has one (Hiroshima's momiji manju maple-leaf cakes, Hokkaido's Shiroi Koibito cookies). Most omiyage are the meibutsu of wherever you went.

**Mechanic:** a shopkeeper explains omiyage while wrapping something, and it clicks: the player has friends in every earlier village. New "Omiyage" inventory tab; collect one meibutsu per past recipient (lemon cakes for the Peru chapter's astronomer, and so on). The game never scores this; NPC reactions are the reward.

## 3. Etiquette Warmly Gotten Wrong

Design rule: mistakes get laughter and gentle correction, never shame.

- **Genkan:** shoes off at the recessed entryway; slippers inside; slippers off again on tatami. Player tracks sand in once; grandmother mock-scolds, then teaches.
- **Bath (sento/onsen):** wash and rinse completely at the seated shower stalls BEFORE entering the shared tub; the tub is for soaking only; the small towel stays out of the water (folded on the head is the classic look).
- **Slurping noodles** is correct and appreciative. Player eats udon silently; old men slurp louder to demonstrate.
- **Two hands:** give and receive things (money, gifts, cups) with both hands; it reads as care.
- **Bowing and aisatsu:** greetings are a social duty and a pleasure; a nod-bow is fine for a visitor, and nobody expects a foreigner to calibrate depth.

## 4. Tanabata: The Star Payoff (July 7)

The player arrives days before **Tanabata**, the Star Festival. The legend: **Orihime**, the weaver princess (the star **Vega**), and **Hikoboshi**, the cowherd (**Altair**), fell so deeply in love they neglected their work. The Sky King separated them across the **Amanogawa**, the "River of Heaven," the Milky Way itself. They may meet only once a year, on the seventh night of the seventh month; in some tellings, if it rains, magpies cannot bridge the river and the lovers wait another year.

People write wishes on **tanzaku**, colored paper strips, and tie them to bamboo, which grows straight toward the heavens; the shotengai hangs bamboo and streamers.

**The payoff:** the player learned the Milky Way in Peru as **Mayu**, Quechua for "river," the celestial river whose earthly twin was the Vilcanota, its water cycling from land to sky and back as rain. Now, an ocean away, an old woman points up: Amanogawa, the river of heaven. Two cultures, no contact, both saw a river. The astronomy checks out: Vega and Altair stand on opposite banks of the Milky Way in the summer sky. (Honest wrinkle for dialogue: July 7 often falls inside the rainy season and the stars get clouded out; some regions hold Tanabata in August for clearer skies.)

## 5. Food

- **Dashi:** the base of everything. Kombu (kelp) and katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes) are the famous pairing, but the Setouchi everyday stock is **iriko dashi** from small dried anchovies; the Inland Sea is a major iriko source, and it is the essential broth for Sanuki udon and rustic miso soup. A grandmother teaching dashi is a perfect quiet scene.
- **Setouchi lemons:** verified. Hiroshima Prefecture grows most of Japan's domestic lemons, centered on Setoda (Ikuchijima and neighboring islands); the slopes there have been called "Lemon Valley" since the 1960s. Lemon cakes make an ideal first omiyage.
- **Shodoshima:** verified. First place in Japan to successfully cultivate olives (from 1908), plus a soy sauce industry dating to the Edo period, with working wooden-barrel breweries today.
- **Onigiri:** rice balls, the boat lunch and ferry snack. **Mikan:** the region's beloved citrus, but the fresh crop peaks in autumn and winter; in July it appears as juice and jelly (respect this seasonality in scripts).

## 6. Language by Ear

No vocabulary UI; phrases repeat in context until the player just knows them. Core set: **ohayō** (morning greeting), **itadakimasu** (before eating), **gochisōsama** (after eating), **sumimasen** (excuse me / thanks / sorry, the all-purpose social oil), **irasshai** (shopkeeper's welcome), **arigatō**, **oyasumi** (goodnight), **ganbatte** (do your best), **tadaima / okaeri** (I'm home / welcome back), **matane** (see you), **oishii** (delicious), **daijōbu** (it's okay). Fisher vocabulary: **tai** (sea bream), **ami** (net), **fune** (boat), **umi** (sea), **tairyō** (big catch).

**Otsukaresama** gets its own beat. Literally "you must be tired," it is untranslatable praise: it acknowledges effort itself, said at the end of work, after a festival, after anything shared. When the harbor crew finally says it to the player after a day hauling nets, it means acceptance.

## 7. Texture and Sound

- **Cicadas:** July is peak season; the wall of sound starts when the rainy season ends. Aburazemi sizzle like frying oil (jiri jiri); minminzemi sing "min min."
- **Tairyō-bata:** "big catch flags," Edo-period banners flown by boats returning with a full hold; designs feature sunrises, fish, treasure ships, and **Ebisu**, the fishing god. Today they mostly fly for boat launchings, New Year, and festivals, so raise ours for Tanabata, not daily.
- **Ebisu shrine:** a small harborside shrine to Ebisu, one of the Seven Lucky Gods and patron of fishermen, is authentic for a fishing port. (Soft claim: our specific shrine's little rituals are invented; Ebisu worship in fishing ports is well documented.)
- Also: geta clacking on stone (soft claim: geta are now mostly festival wear, so tie the sound to Tanabata night), **amado** rain shutters rattling shut before a storm, kei trucks idling at the port at dawn, ferry horns, noren lifting in sea wind.

## 8. Traps to Avoid

- No Kyoto/Tokyo bleed: no geisha, no packed crossings, no temple-city grandeur. Rural western Japan is informal, teasing, direct; a fishing port runs on banter and nicknames, not keigo.
- No samurai, ninja, or anime references; no tea ceremony as daily habit (tea is just tea here).
- Rainy season reality: early July can be humid and wet until tsuyu ends mid-month; use it.
- Don't make every elder wise and serene; let one be cranky about the art tourists.
